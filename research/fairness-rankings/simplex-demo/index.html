How the estimate moved

The fairness test — evidence as a martingale

Reading the panels

Why the triangle

The weights are normalised so ωA + ωB + ωC = 1, which makes the parameter space a triangle. Every panel is a density over that triangle, evaluated on a pixel lattice rather than sampled — what you see is the exact surface, not a smoothed cloud of draws.

What α does

The prior is symmetric — before seeing the ranking, no group is assumed favoured — so α controls only how tightly it clusters on the fair centre. α = 1 is flat over the whole triangle; large α concentrates the prior on fairness and so bets on mild departures from it, which makes the evidence grow more slowly against a badly skewed ranking; α < 1 pushes mass out to the edges and corners. It is a real modelling choice, not a nuisance: it is the bet the auditor places before looking.

Why the likelihood keeps changing shape

The second panel is ωc · rc / (ω · r) — the chance that this particular group won this particular draw. It depends on r, the candidates still available, so the same group drawn twice contributes different evidence at different depths. Drawing from a group that is nearly exhausted is strong evidence of a high weight; drawing from a group that dominates what is left says almost nothing. This dependence on r is the whole of sampling without replacement.

Two procedures, one number

Switch the procedure at the top and the curve does not move — because Et and BF10 are the same quantity, mt / Lf,t. What changes is the decision rule attached to it, and the region drawn on the posterior. SAVI compares against a single threshold 1/δ = 20 and earns the right to be checked after every position; the Bayesian reading grades the same value on the Kass–Raftery scale at a prefix that was supposed to be fixed in advance. The regions genuinely differ: the confidence sequence is a superlevel set of the likelihood at δ·mt, the HDI a superlevel set of the posterior holding 95% of its mass.

Yes, the anytime-valid version is conservative

Visibly so, and it is worth being precise about how much. Simulating 400 rankings from a genuinely fair ω on the default pool with α = 2:

under a fair truth measured nominal
SAVI rejects at some draw 1.3%≤ 5%
confidence sequence covers at every draw 98.5%≥ 95%
95% HDI covers at the final draw 96.5%≈ 95%

So the test spends only about a quarter of its error budget, and the confidence sequence over-covers by three and a half points where the HDI over-covers by one and a half. Three things cause it, and only the first is avoidable:

The prior dilutes the evidence. mt averages the likelihood over the whole simplex, including the regions the data have already ruled out. A prior spread thin over parameter values nobody is testing for drags the average down and the Bayes factor with it. Narrowing α toward the alternative you actually care about recovers real power — this is a modelling choice, not a fixed cost.

Ville's inequality is loose. P(supt Et ≥ 1/δ) ≤ δ is tight only for a martingale that either reaches the boundary exactly or drifts to zero. A real e-process overshoots when it crosses and frequently never approaches, so the true rate sits well under the bound.

Time-uniformity is genuinely expensive. Covering at all 50 prefixes at once is a far stronger demand than covering at one chosen prefix, and the confidence sequence pays for it in width. That part is not slack — it is the guarantee.

The honest summary: if you truly fix k in advance, the Bayesian region is tighter and you should use it. If you want to watch the ranking fill and stop when convinced, the extra width is the price of that freedom, and it is smaller than the alternative — peeking at a p-value with no correction pushes the error rate to roughly 23% at a nominal 5%.

What to try

Put the truth at the centre and watch the posterior stay diffuse and the evidence wander around 1: a fair ranking simply carries little information about which group is favoured, and the martingale has nowhere to climb. Then push the truth toward the corner of the smallest group. Rare groups are the most informative — a 5-candidate group appearing early is very unlikely under fairness — so the posterior sharpens fast and the evidence crosses 20 within a few positions. Finally set α = 30 and sample again: the same ranking now earns much less evidence, because the alternative you bet on barely admits the skew you are trying to detect.
